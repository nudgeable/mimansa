Why measure potential at all

· · ·

The problem is not that companies use performance. The problem is that they use the same evidence, add a subjective potential label and present it as a separate insight.

If performance is the evidence we actually trust, improve how we measure it:

  • Measure it more often: Use evidence gathered throughout the year rather than one annual conversation.
  • Make it transparent: Let employees understand the criteria and the evidence behind the judgement.
  • Test it across contexts: Look at performance across different assignments, challenges, teams and managers.
  • Include more perspectives: Use input from stakeholders and 360 feedback instead of relying on one manager's opinion.

Who says every organization must measure potential? There is no law requiring it. If we cannot define potential consistently, predict it accurately or separate it from performance, we should stop creating processes that pretend otherwise.

We do not need another tool for measuring potential. We need a better and more transparent record of performance across time and contexts, and the honesty to use it for what it is: our best available estimate of future performance.

So what do leaders mean when they say potential? Let's hear what they answer.

Ask five leaders to define potential

You will probably hear ambition, learning speed, executive presence or willingness to take on more responsibility.

Assessment providers are not aligned either. Korn Ferry focuses on learning agility, while Gartner uses ability, aspiration and engagement. Other providers emphasize drive, ambition or cognitive ability. Companies choose one framework and proceed as though the question has been resolved. They have simply chosen one vocabulary over another.

Frameworks namedKorn Ferry · Gartner

Once the branded language is removed, most frameworks come down to three broad qualities:

  • Cognitive ability: How quickly someone understands complexity and handles unfamiliar problems.
  • Drive: Whether the person voluntarily takes on harder work and persists when it becomes uncomfortable.
  • Social skill: Whether other people trust the person and are willing to follow their lead.

Even these are difficult to assess fairly. Drive is often confused with visibility, long working hours, willingness to travel or proximity to senior leaders. Someone who speaks frequently in meetings may appear to have greater potential than someone quietly handling much harder work.

This system came from a very different world

The formal assessment of potential has its roots in the Second World War. In 1942, the British Army introduced War Office Selection Boards to select officers from a large population of people it did not know well. Candidates completed tests, interviews, group exercises and command simulations.

1942British Army War Office Selection Boards

In 1956, Douglas Bray adapted the approach for AT&T's Management Progress Study. AT&T was a stable, regulated company with defined career ladders, and the study followed managers for around twenty years.

1956Douglas Bray, AT&T Management Progress Study
~20 yrshow long the study followed managers

Today, employees may leave within three or four years. Roles and reporting lines change quickly. We are using a long-term forecasting method created for stable careers in organizations where neither the roles nor the people remain stable for very long.

3–4 yrshow soon employees may leave today, per the author

The nine-box format added another layer of formality. The original GE-McKinsey matrix was developed in the early 1970s to decide which business units deserved investment. It was later adapted for people because it looked structured.

1970sGE-McKinsey matrix, built to rank business units

Assessment centres, competency models and the nine-box grid made potential assessment look scientific. Very few organizations asked how much these methods could actually predict.

We remove people from their context and then assess them

When organizations want a formal assessment of potential, assessment centres are among the most commonly used methods. An employee is taken away from their actual role and placed inside a simulation. Assessors observe them for a few hours and use that behavior to predict how they might perform in a larger role several years later.

Assessment centres remove people from the context in which their performance was created. Performance is influenced by the manager, team, authority, organizational culture, relationships and knowledge of the business. The same person may perform very differently under another manager or inside another team.

Someone who is confident in simulations may score well. Another employee may do their best work after understanding a problem deeply and building trust. The assessment centre is likely to reward the first person because that behavior is easier to observe within a few hours.

Assessment centres are also weak predictors of how well someone will perform in a real job.Their scores explain only around 8% of the differences in later job performance.A high score does not guarantee strong performance, and a low score does not prove someone will perform poorly.

~8%of later job performance explained by assessment centre scores

HR does not automatically make the process objective

HR sometimes challenges a business leader's assessment. The problem is that the HR representative may have interacted with the employee for fifteen minutes during the entire year. Their view may come from one meeting, second-hand feedback or the employee's general reputation.

15 mintime HR may have spent with the employee all year, per the author

Written evidence has limits too. Once someone has decided that a favorite employee has potential, they are likely to find examples supporting that conclusion. Writing the evidence down improves the discussion, but it does not remove favoritism or confirmation bias.

Performance becomes a substitute for potential

The nine-box grid presents performance and potential as separate dimensions. In practice, people use performance as a proxy for potential almost every time.

I have rarely seen someone rated low on performance and high on potential. Leaders cannot define potential clearly, so they use the evidence they understand, which is current performance. The nine-box grid often measures performance twice: once directly and once under the label of potential.

This can also become a self-fulfilling prophecy. Managers give more support and opportunities to employees they rate high on potential. Those employees get more chances to develop and demonstrate their abilities. Their success then appears to confirm the original rating, while others have fewer opportunities to prove themselves.

Why measure potential at all

This raises an obvious question: if companies already use past performance as a proxy for future performance, is that truly a bad thing? I do not think it is.

Past performance, observed repeatedly across different situations, is a reasonable indicator of future performance. It is based on actual work over time, not behavior during a few hours of simulation. It will never predict an unfamiliar role perfectly, but it can provide a much more informed judgement than a separate potential score that nobody can define consistently.

The problem is not that companies use performance. The problem is that they use the same evidence, add a subjective potential label and present it as a separate insight.

If performance is the evidence we actually trust, improve how we measure it:

  • Measure it more often: Use evidence gathered throughout the year rather than one annual conversation.
  • Make it transparent: Let employees understand the criteria and the evidence behind the judgement.
  • Test it across contexts: Look at performance across different assignments, challenges, teams and managers.
  • Include more perspectives: Use input from stakeholders and 360 feedback instead of relying on one manager's opinion.

Who says every organization must measure potential? There is no law requiring it. If we cannot define potential consistently, predict it accurately or separate it from performance, we should stop creating processes that pretend otherwise.

We do not need another tool for measuring potential. We need a better and more transparent record of performance across time and contexts, and the honesty to use it for what it is: our best available estimate of future performance.
