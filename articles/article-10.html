Today's question: why do companies write down their values, and then not live by them? Over to the author.

I was surprised to see a poster displaying company values at my bike service centre in Mumbai. I had associated org values with large organisations, but apparently even a service centre now needed to tell customers what it stood for.

Those values made no noticeable difference to the service I received, and when I casually asked the staff what their company values were, they had no idea. It made me wonder how defining values had become so common when so little attention was being paid to practising them.

What has actually changed?

Business pressure tests whether leaders will follow their stated values. Correcting a customer’s bill costs money, and confronting a senior leader about misconduct can create conflict. Companies must be prepared to accept those consequences if they expect employees to take their values seriously.

Now that even bike service centres have org values, what matters is how honestly and how frequently employees are living these values.

Values in action: The Taj during 26/11

Values in action · The Taj, 26/11

During the November 2008 terrorist attacks, employees at the Taj stayed to help guests despite knowing the danger they faced. They knew the hotel’s exits, yet continued assisting people through the siege, and some lost their lives while helping guests.

Harvard Business School examined their response alongside the Taj’s approach to recruitment, training and recognition. Its account described an organisation that consistently reinforced responsibility towards guests through its management practices.

The employees’ extraordinary courage cannot be attributed to publishing their company values, but to living them each day through small actions.

The author goes back to the 1990s to find out. Let's follow.

How did defining values become popular?

But the bestselling book Built to Last, by Jim Collins and Jerry Porras, published in 1994, claimed that defining values was the key to lasting organisational success, and it used examples of some large companies like J&J and its Credo.

SourceCollins & Porras (1994), Built to Last.

This became a trend, and soon management consultants picked this up as a service they provided to organisations.

But not every business owner displaying values has read these books. Companies also copy competitors. If your competitor has customer service as a core value, how can you not?

Defining values has become something organisations do because they believe a professionally managed company is expected to have them.

Why do companies want values?

The first reason is that everyone else has them. When competitors publish their mission, vision and values, leaders want their own. The leaders want something to talk about in media interviews.

The second reason is that values support company positioning. They provide material for recruitment campaigns, media articles, leadership presentations and communication around workplace recognition such as GPTW. Leaders can describe the company as principled, customer-focused or committed to employees without explaining what those commitments require them to do. The company wants to show that it is principled and does not compromise…it is to show itself in a positive light. Values become part of all the careers pages on its website and its investor reports.

The third reason is that companies need to send a message when results are achieved in the wrong ways. A salesperson can exceed targets while misleading customers, bullying colleagues or making commitments the company cannot deliver. Written behavioural expectations give managers a basis for challenging that conduct and imposing consequences.

ExampleThe salesperson who hits target the wrong way

This is a legitimate use of values. Employees need to know that achieving their targets does not excuse unacceptable behaviour, and managers need to include that behaviour in performance decisions.

Why does so little change?

Enron listed integrity among its values and used “Ask Why” as its advertising slogan. Those declarations did not prevent the conduct that led to its collapse.

Four steps follow. Notice who has to act in each one.

Step 1 of 4

1. Identify behaviours relevant to your business.

Study how employees deliver good work responsibly, including the difficult decisions they make and the conduct that helps others succeed. These examples provide a more useful starting point than copying another company’s values.

Step 2 of 4

2. Explain what each value requires employees to do.

At my service centre, customer focus should mean providing an accurate estimate, seeking permission before additional repairs and explaining mistakes honestly. Even a small thing like asking customers if they would like a cup of tea when they visit the centre has an impact. Employees should understand these expectations clearly enough to apply them without needing HR to interpret the value.

ToolValues One-PagerWrite down what each value asks people to do.
Step 3 of 4

3. Make behaviour count in performance decisions.

Values must influence bonuses, promotions and disciplinary action. Someone who repeatedly mistreats colleagues should face consequences even when they deliver strong results, and senior leaders must be held to the same standards.

Exempting powerful employees makes the entire exercise lose credibility.

Step 4 of 4

4. Reinforce the expectations continuously.

Managers should discuss actual examples, including misleading sales they rejected, mistakes employees disclosed and customer complaints handled properly. New employees need to learn these expectations, while existing employees need to see them consistently applied. Stories about these values spread far and are more memorable.

An annual presentation cannot achieve this.
