The Exit Interview: A Ritual of Polite Lies

We built a confession booth, and put it in a culture with every reason never to confess

At Mimansa we ask the questions that have been sitting in the room for years. HR spends its life asking everyone else the hard questions. This time, the questions are for HR.

Would you tell your employer the real reason you left, while you still needed a reference?

Think about when we ask. The offer letter is signed. The notice period has started. Someone has already ordered the farewell cake. And that's when we sit the person down and ask, "So, why are you really leaving?"

And, we build a dashboard to analyse whatever they say.

I've spent years designing HR processes and setting up HRMS systems, and along the way I've looked at exit interview data from more than 800 organisations. The top 3 reasons hardly ever change:

800+organisations' exit data the author has seen
  • Better pay
  • Better role
  • Personal reasons

Look at that list again. You can't act on any of it. It doesn't point at anyone. And most of the time, it isn't the real reason. It's what people say once they've already left in their head and just want to leave nicely.

It was never meant to hear the truth

When did your exit data last change a single decision?

The exit interview isn't some timeless HR wisdom. It started in America in the 1950s. It was openly called a public-relations tool, and a way for companies to check whether they'd hired the right people in the first place.

1950swhere the exit interview began, as a public-relations tool

So from the very start, it was there to protect the company, not to tell it uncomfortable things about itself.

Today about 90% of Fortune 500 companies still run exit interviews. Only around half of them say it actually works. Researchers have doubted the answers since 1969, when Lefkowitz and Katz first questioned them. The reason is something every HR person already knows. People who are leaving care more about their friends at work, their references and their name in the industry than about telling you what really happened.

The last reason is never the real one

This is what the top-3 list hides.

Researchers Lee and Mitchell found that people don't resign in 1 moment for 1 reason. It builds up. It starts with something that hurts. A promotion goes to someone else. A manager talks down to you in a meeting. A promise isn't kept. A close friend at work leaves. Or you just feel nobody sees you.

ResearchLee and Mitchell, on how people decide to leave

Each time, the same thought comes back: Should I still be here? People carry that thought for months before they even update their CV.

By the time they resign, the new offer is just the last straw. The exit interview asks about the last straw and calls it the reason.

I've seen something similar in counselling conversations. When people are struggling, they almost never point to 1 incident. They talk about a string of small things, often mentioned in passing, long before anyone around them noticed. What makes the difference is whether someone heard those early signs in time.

People decide to leave months before they resign. Who is listening in month one?

Resignations are like that too. The signs were there. We just weren't listening while it still mattered.

The Sita problem

Our own epics have a story about an exit that looked sudden but wasn't.

सीताThe parallel · Ramayana

Sita had already walked through fire to prove herself. She was sent away. She raised her 2 sons alone in a forest ashram. And then she was asked to prove her purity all over again.

She didn't argue. She didn't complain. She didn't explain all the years that led to that moment. She asked Bhoomi Devi, Mother Earth, to take her back. And the earth opened up and took her.

If someone had stopped Sita on her way out and asked why she was leaving, the honest answer wouldn't have been the 2nd test. It would have been everything before it, carried in silence, until 1 more demand was too much. She didn't fight. She didn't explain. She just left.

Every polite exit interview that tells you nothing is a small version of that same goodbye.
