Today's question: why do we ask people why they're leaving, in the week they leave? Over to the author.

We ask people why they're leaving on their last week, when they have every reason to lie and none to tell the truth. And then we build dashboards on the answer.

In my career of designing processes and implementing HRMS, I have come across data on Exit interviews for more than 800 organisations, across the globe. Across organisations, the top three reasons for exit are almost always the same: better compensation, better role, personal reasons. The common thread, none of them are actionable, none of them implicate anyone, and none of them are usually the real reason. They are the answer given by someone who has already mentally left and is now only managing the optics of leaving.

The question it leaves

So, Are we running the exit interview because it produces insight, or because it lets us close the file and tell ourselves we asked?

We sit someone down in their last week — after the offer is signed, the notice period is running, the goodbyes have half-started — and ask them, on record, why they're really leaving. Then we build a dashboard on the answer.

In my career of designing processes and implementing HRMS, I have come across data on Exit interviews for more than 800 organisations, across the globe. Across organisations, the top three reasons for exit are almost always the same: better compensation, better role, personal reasons. The common thread, none of them are actionable, none of them implicate anyone, and none of them are usually the real reason. They are the answer given by someone who has already mentally left and is now only managing the optics of leaving. In one detailed case study of exit interviews at a multinational's Asia-Pacific office, researchers found employees followed a near-universal principle in their answers: make sure you don't burn any bridges. Not "tell the truth." Not "help the next person." Burn no bridges. That was the actual operating instruction, consistently, across every departing employee interviewed.

Question 1 of 3

Is it Inertia ?

The exit interview isn't ancient HR wisdom; it's a mid-century American invention. The earliest literature on it surfaces around the 1950s, and its original purpose wasn't even employee wellbeing; it was framed explicitly as a public-relations tool and a way to check the soundness of the company's own hiring decisions. It was built, from day one, to protect the organisation's image and validate its own judgment, not primarily to surface uncomfortable truths about itself.

1950swhere the exit interview began, as a public-relations tool

That origin has aged into a strange present. Today, roughly 90% of Fortune 500 companies run exit interviews. Only about half of them report finding the process actually successful. Academic researchers going back to the 1960s — Lefkowitz and Katz in 1969, later Zarandona and Camuso, have repeatedly questioned the validity of what gets collected, for a reason every HR professional already knows in their gut: a departing employee's "allegiance to remaining co-workers" and their own future reputation in the market shapes what they say far more than the truth does.

Question 2 of 3

The real reason was never the last reason

Here is the part the top-three-reasons list obscures completely. Researchers most notably Lee and Mitchell, in what's known academically as the "unfolding model of voluntary turnover" worked out decades ago that resignation is not a single event triggered by a single cause. It is a process, set off by one or more "shocks": a passed-over promotion, a disrespectful meeting, a missed commitment, a peer's exit, a moment where someone felt unseen. Each shock reopens a mental script the person runs — should I still be here — sometimes for months, long before any job search even begins. By the time the resignation letter is typed, the "final" reason — a competing offer, say is usually just the last domino, not the first one. The exit interview asks about the domino that fell last and calls it the cause.

SourceLee & Mitchell (1994), the unfolding model of voluntary turnover, Academy of Management Review.

भूमिThe parallel · Ramayana

Indian mythology has its own account of an exit that looks sudden but wasn't. When Sita is asked, a second time, to prove her fidelity, after banishment, after raising her sons alone in a forest hermitage, after an entire life of quiet endurance already spent proving herself once, she doesn't argue her case. She doesn't list her grievances. She doesn't stay to explain the years that led to this moment. She asks Bhoomi Devi, the earth, to take her back, and the earth opens and does. If you'd interviewed Sita on her way out and asked "why are you leaving," the honest answer was never the second test alone. It was everything that came before it, silently absorbed, until one more small demand became one demand too many. She didn't burn a bridge on her way out. She simply left, with no explanation offered to the very institution that had asked her, repeatedly, to keep proving herself. Every organisation that's ever received a beautifully polite, entirely uninformative exit interview has just been handed a smaller, quieter version of the same departure.
