Coddled, or Just Unwilling to Perform Silence?

Gen Z keeps asking "why." Maybe the real question is why we stopped.

At Mimansa we ask the questions that have been sitting in the room for years. HR spends its life asking everyone else the hard questions. This time, the questions are for HR.

When a new joiner asks “why”, is your first instinct to explain, or to manage them?

We were at a team lunch, swapping stories about our more colourful clients, when one of our new joiners turned to me.

"Himanshu, don't you think our system is hard to use? I've spent 2 months on it, all day, every day, and I still get stuck."

The table went silent. A few people looked at their plates.

My first thought, I'm embarrassed to say, was: maybe the problem is you. That's how I was raised at work. If something is hard, look inward. Fix yourself first.

Then a second thought arrived. If someone who uses our system all day finds it this hard, what are our customers going through?

My new colleague had just handed me a free customer insight. And my first instinct was to blame them for it.

A generation we love to label

Every Millennial manager I know has a Gen Z story. The intern who asked "why" in a team meeting. The analyst who pushed back on a deadline. The new hire who said, plainly, that a process made no sense.

We have names for this. "Coddled." "Entitled." "Can't take feedback."

I've managed Gen Z long enough to see a different picture. Yes, some of them are difficult. Every generation has a few. But most of what we file under "can't take feedback" is something else: a generation that simply skipped the training in staying silent that we spent our careers perfecting.

The harder question

Is Gen Z bad at hard truths, or are we out of practice at telling them?

So here's the harder version. Is Gen Z bad at hearing hard truths? Or are Millennials, the generation now doing most of the managing and most of the complaining, just out of practice at telling them?

Think about what a Millennial career in India looked like, especially in the first 10 years. We accepted the appraisal rating, the onboarding, the training, as given. When a decision seemed wrong, we executed it anyway. Partly out of self-doubt. Partly because asking "why" looked like insubordination, and insubordination had a price our families could ill afford.

ContextA Millennial career in India, the first 10 years

For many of us who joined the Indian workforce in the 2000s, job security was the whole point of the job. Hierarchy, predictability and respect for the person above us were the operating system. If you wanted to keep your seat, you ran on it.

ContextJoining the Indian workforce in the 2000s

Now that same generation manages a cohort that runs on a different operating system. And our reflex is to call the new one broken, rather than ask whether the old one was ever healthy.

I learnt this the hard way. In 2012, at EY, I felt my rating was unfair. The idealistic young HR consultant in me went further and proposed changing the system, because I believed it was measuring the wrong things. My ideas were laughed off. In a closed room, a senior leader called me a rebel and a "union leader." My colleagues advised me to let it go, and eventually I did. It seemed pointless to create so much noise over something I was powerless to change.

Today, when my own team asks me similar questions, I feel the same discomfort that leader must have felt. The old instinct still kicks in. But I've learnt to pause, and listen for the real question underneath.

What the research says

The popular story and the data tell very different tales.

A 2026 workplace study found that 97% of Gen Z employees are open to ongoing feedback, and 63% prefer regular, timely feedback over a once-a-year review. Studies from 2025 found that Gen Z responds well to direct, specific criticism. What they push back on is vague praise, and criticism that arrives too late to act on.

So the familiar complaint that Gen Z "needs constant feedback" actually describes a workforce asking for more honesty, more often. That sounds like the opposite of coddled.

The same research is less flattering to us. In earlier studies of what each generation wants from a manager, Millennials ranked "open communication and feedback" at the top. We wanted exactly this kind of directness from our own bosses, and most of us received very little of it.

We wanted the very thing we're now being asked to give. We're simply better at wanting it than at giving it.

In India, the gap is even wider. On Hofstede's power distance index, which measures how much a society accepts unequal authority, India scores 77. The United States scores 40. In Indian organisations, feedback has always flowed easily downward and rarely upward.

I still remember the first time my manager at EY asked me for feedback. I was so taken aback that I was lost for words.

The Nachiketa problem

Asking for feedback is a skill too. When did you last ask your team?

Nachiketa turned down every comfort for one honest answer. Who in your team would?

नचिकेताThe parallel · Katha Upanishad

The Katha Upanishad tells the story of Nachiketa, a boy his own father angrily gave away to Yama, the god of death.

Nachiketa waits at Yama's door for 3 days and nights without food or welcome. To make amends, Yama grants him 3 boons. For his 3rd, Nachiketa asks the one question he really came for: what happens after death?

Yama tries everything to avoid answering. He offers wealth beyond measure, a long life, every pleasure a person could want. Nachiketa turns down every offer. He wants the truth, and he's prepared to wait for it.

And Yama, the highest authority there is, respects that persistence enough to finally answer honestly.

Now look at the story again and ask who is being coddled. The one seeking comfort is Yama. He offers gifts to escape a hard conversation.

ToolPeer Observation SheetNotice when reassurance takes the place of an honest answer.

That may be closer to our own 1-on-1s than we'd like to admit. A young employee asks a plain, hard question. And the manager, uncomfortable, offers reassurance, a new title, an exciting project, anything except the honest answer the question deserved.

So when a Gen Z employee questions a decision instead of simply executing it, it lands on a generation that spent 15 years earning the right to give orders without explanation. It may feel like disrespect. It may also be the first real test, in Indian workplaces, of an assumption we lacked the standing to test ourselves.
