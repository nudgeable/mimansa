Today's question: is AI-made work making us trust each other less? Over to the author.

AI slop is everywhere on social media. Posts that say nothing. Images that look impressive until you look closely. Videos produced because someone can produce them, rather than because they have something to say.

Do you think this is limited to our personal lives?

It is happening at work too.

I recently spoke to a CEO who runs a Global Capability Centre (GCC) in India. Every week, his team prepares a report that he sends to senior leaders in the US. He was worried that his team might be using AI without reviewing the output properly. He did not want to send AI workslop under his name.

His question was simple: how could he tell whether the report was good without reading the whole thing carefully?

Why does this create mistrust?

When a colleague sends you a report, you assume they have thought through it. You may disagree with their conclusions, but you expect them to understand and explain their work.

AI makes that assumption harder to hold.

A strong report and a weak report can look almost identical. Both have clean slides, confident headings and fluent English. You discover the difference only when you read closely or ask questions.

Question 5 of 5

What can companies actually do?

HR could start with one recurring report that employees complain takes too long to read. Work with that team to introduce a few rules:

  • A 300-word weekly update: what changed, what needs attention and what decision is required. Supporting detail goes in an appendix.
  • Sources beside important numbers: the reader should not have to chase the sender for them.
  • A specific recommendation: what should happen next, who should do it and by when.
  • A short walkthrough when needed: ask the sender to explain the recommendation and the evidence behind it.

A report may have 20 slides, yet you finish without knowing what happened or what the team recommends.

20slides, and still no clear recommendation

Two years ago, preparing a presentation took human effort. Someone had to decide what to include, write the sentences and assemble the slides. Weak reports existed, but producing them still took time.

Now, someone who needed days can produce a presentation in an hour. The output looks better, but their understanding has not necessarily changed.

The work can look more capable than the person’s understanding of it.

There is another problem. Someone who previously put effort into a thoughtful presentation could stand out. Now everyone can produce polished slides.

If managers cannot distinguish the thinking behind those presentations, will the people who previously did excellent work keep putting in that effort? If an unchecked AI presentation gets the same appreciation, what are we encouraging?

Recruitment has a similar problem. AI makes CVs sound impressive, leaving recruiters to find the actual experience behind the language. Some use AI to shortlist CVs that candidates used AI to write.

ExampleRecruitment and AI-written CVs

Question 4 of 5

Will better AI skills solve this?

Some workslop comes from a capability gap. People do not yet know how to give AI enough context, question its answers or improve its output. Practice and training should help.

But knowing how to use AI and being willing to check its work are different things.

Someone can be excellent at prompting and still forward a report they have barely read. If that report gets accepted, there is little reason to change.

Mistrust will not disappear simply because AI becomes familiar. It will reduce when colleagues repeatedly receive work they can rely on.
