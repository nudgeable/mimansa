If you scrapped the review cycle tomorrow, who in the business would miss it?

Every year, in calibration rooms across India, the same scene plays out. A manager defends a rating for work they never really tracked. The evidence is a form the employee filled in 12 months late. The audience is a committee that saw none of it. Everyone knows it isn't working. Everyone signs off anyway.

Only 2% of Fortune 500 CHROs strongly agree that their own performance system inspires people to improve. 95% of managers are unhappy with it. We've redesigned it for 70 years. The outcome hasn't moved.

70 years of moving the problem

Every fix in 70 years solved one problem and created the next. What did your last redesign create?

The appraisal wasn't born in business. It was born in the army. And every fix since has solved one problem by creating the next.

  • 1914–1945 · US Army rating scales Fixed: no fair way to sort soldiers for promotion. Created: a system designed to eliminate, not to develop. Corporate America adopted it after the war, and by the 1960s about 90% of US employers had one. Indian companies inherited it a generation later.
  • 1954 · Drucker's Management by Objectives Fixed: goals handed down from the top. Now they would be agreed. Created: once pay was tied to MBO scores, people negotiated soft targets instead of stretch ones.
  • 1970s · Andy Grove's OKRs at Intel Fixed: soft targets, by building a hard wall between goals and pay. Created: most companies that adopted OKRs later tore that wall down and tied them back to pay.
  • 1980s · Jack Welch's forced ranking at GE Fixed: rating inflation, where everyone "exceeded expectations." Created: 20% at the top, 70% in the middle, and the bottom 10% out, whether the team deserved it or not. Colleagues became competitors.
  • 1990s–2000s · The bell curve comes to India Fixed: rating lakhs of people consistently in a pyramid-shaped billing model. ICICI Bank, ONGC, SAIL and almost all of large-cap IT ran it for nearly 20 years. Created: people fought the curve instead of the competition. And the best people left.
  • 2012–2016 · The great unranking Fixed: the curve. Adobe, Deloitte, Accenture and GE dropped rankings. In 2015 Infosys moved to absolute ratings, and TCS and Wipro followed within a year. Created: rating inflation came back, the very problem Welch set out to solve. And since the increment budget was still fixed, the calibration room rebuilt the curve anyway. It just stopped calling it one.
  • Today · Continuous feedback and OKR platforms Fixed: the once-a-year conversation. Created: more check-ins, logged in a system the business doesn't use to run anything.

I was in some of those rooms when Indian IT dropped the bell curve. The reasons were almost word for word what American companies had said years earlier. We consultants picked up redesign mandates in the same breath. An industry was paying a fortune to undo a system it had paid a fortune to build 10 years before.

Why the business doesn't own it

Sales reviews its pipeline every week. Operations checks its targets every shift. Finance closes the books every month. Every function manages performance all year, as part of running the business. None of them does it in SuccessFactors, Workday or Darwinbox.

Then HR shows up in March with a form. It asks the same leaders to do it all again, in a different system, in a different language, for a different audience. Not the customer. Not the P&L. The calibration committee.

Of course the business treats it as separate. It is separate. What HR runs in March isn't performance management. It's pay allocation and paperwork, wearing performance management's name. Leaders sense it, which is why they finish the form in 20 minutes and go back to the dashboard that actually runs their business.

I ran a business function for nearly 10 years. The gap starts at goal-setting. The goals in the system aren't the outcomes the business actually tracks. HR either has no say, or hands out 1 template for everything, whether the goal is tracked weekly for a quarter or needs milestones over several years.
