Every organization insists performance management exists to drive performance. Ask employees if it does, and the numbers embarrass the premise. In nearly two decades of my consulting career with Indian and APAC organisations on performance process redesign, I've watched most of them reach for the same shelf: MBO, Balanced Scorecard, OKRs, Continuous feedback; Western philosophies, imported wholesale. I have asked, many times, what I suspect many others have quietly asked too: does an appraisal need to be designed differently for organisations with different culture, context and industry ? I rarely stopped to ask before I implemented.

But the question has remained with me for over a decade, and I keep trying to decipher, as to what is the system trying to solve for, really - Provide a legal cover against probable bias, or a Cover up for Manager incapability on effective conversations or a Utopian Hope to connect a string of KPIs to Real business outcomes.

There is a beautiful story in Indian mythology on what happened when KPI was measured against the wrong perception of the outcome. Shiva and Parvati offer a fruit of wisdom to whichever of their two sons can circle the universe first. Kartikeya takes it literally: he mounts his peacock and sets off on an exhausting, genuine, full-effort journey around the entire physical cosmos. Ganesha, reasoning that his parents are the universe, simply walks around them once and claims the fruit before Kartikeya has even finished. When Kartikeya returns, spent, having given everything he had to the task as he understood it; he finds the outcome already decided, and not in his favour. Nobody disputes that Kartikeya's effort was real, total, and sincere. It simply wasn't aimed at what the evaluator was actually measuring. In my experience, this is almost the universal complaint heard in every calibration room in the country; an employee who worked hardest by their own definition of the goal (Highest self rating), evaluated at year-end against a definition of "outcome" they were never actually given, and rated significantly lower. Question 1 of 2

Why does the fix keeps not fixing the problem ?

Kyun

A short history first. Count how many times the system was rebuilt.

The oldest ancestor of the appraisal isn't corporate at all, it's the U.S. Army's WWI and WWII rating scales, built to sort soldiers for promotion or discharge. Its DNA was compliance and elimination, not growth. Corporate America adopted it wholesale after the war; by the 1960s, roughly 90% of U.S. employers had one Indian subsidiaries of multinationals, followed by India's own emerging corporate sector, inherited the same DNA a generation later.

~90%of U.S. employers had an appraisal by the 1960s

In 1954, Peter Drucker's Management by Objectives proposed that goals be agreed, not handed down. But once increments got tied to MBO scores, employees learned to negotiate soft targets instead of stretch ones. The 1980s gave the world Jack Welch's forced ranking at GE; a 20-70-10 curve that removed a fixed bottom decile whether the team deserved it or not. India adopted this version with unusual enthusiasm: ICICI Bank, several PSUs including ONGC and SAIL, and most visibly, the entire large-cap IT services industry ran bell curves for the better part of two decades, largely because a strict forced distribution was administratively convenient for organisations managing hundreds of thousands of employees across a rigid, pyramid-shaped billing and utilization model.

1954Drucker's Management by Objectives
20-70-10GE's forced-ranking curve, 1980s

I was myself part of some of the discussions with top HR leaders when Infosys scrapped the bell curve for "iCount" in 2015, and TCS and Wipro followed within a year. The reason given was almost word-for-word what American companies had said years earlier; it killed collaboration, it felt arbitrary, and it was quietly driving attrition of exactly the people it was meant to retain. We, as consultants, picked up new mandates redesigning performance management and rewards distribution in the very same breath and quietly noted the irony of an industry redesigning, at great expense, the very system it had insisted on building at equally great expense a decade earlier.

Running in parallel, Andy Grove at Intel rebuilt Drucker's MBO around Key Results and a hard wall between goal-tracking and pay, precisely to prevent the evils MBO had invited. John Doerr carried the framework to a thirty-person Google in 1999, and OKRs became the default language of ambition everywhere that wanted to look like Silicon Valley including a large share of India's startup and technology sector today, adopted with the same imported enthusiasm, and largely the same absence of anyone asking whether a 100 -year-old Tech manufacturing company needed the framework applied differently than a 22-year-old IT company in Mountain View.

Question 2 of 2

Why does the business treat this as HR's exercise, not its own

Here is the part, history doesn't fully explain. In all the organisations that I have worked with, Sales runs a live pipeline dashboard, reviewed weekly, sometimes daily. Operations runs against a production target or a project milestone that it checks every shift/ every week. Finance closes the books every month. Every core business function manages its own performance continuously, as part of running the business. Never, ever it’s the Performance management platform (likes of Successfactors/Workday/Darwinbox). Then, once or twice a year, HR arrives with a form and asks the same business leaders who have been managing performance in real time all year to do it, in the HRMS, in a different vocabulary, for a different audience: the calibration committee, not the customer or the P&L.

Is it any wonder the business treats it as separate? It genuinely is separate, separate cadence, separate owner, separate purpose. The business's real performance management happens in the Monday pipeline review and the monthly ops call. What HR runs in March is not that. It is a compensation-allocation and documentation exercise wearing performance management's name and most business leaders can tell the difference even when they can't articulate it, which is exactly why they fill it out in twenty minutes and go back to the dashboard that actually runs their business.

In my own experience running a business function for nearly a decade, and watching business leaders across other organisations do the same, the pattern repeats with almost no variation. The biggest gap, the one that cascades down, is at goal-setting. The goals set are not the real outcomes a business is tracking. Either HR has no role in setting them, or HR issues process guidelines on how to set goals that mean nothing to the specific business; the same template applied whether the outcome in question is tracked weekly for a quarter or is a multi-year goal that needed interim milestones, not a single year-end number. And at the end of it all comes the calibration discussion, where business leaders painstakingly justify ratings to HR for outcomes HR was never tracking, armed with nothing but a form the employee filled in, describing, a year later, what they believe they did.

Ask this plainly: does your organisation's business leadership see the annual review cycle as how performance gets managed, or as a compliance step that happens after performance has already been managed, all year, somewhere else? If it's the second and for most companies, it is, then HR isn't failing to run performance management well. HR is running the only cycle nobody in the business actually uses to manage anything.
