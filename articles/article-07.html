Today's question: is a performance improvement plan really a plan to help someone improve? Over to the author.

I have worked in HR for years, and I have seen hardly any employees come out of a performance improvement plan with their job intact. In my experience, roughly nine out of ten either resigned or were eventually asked to leave.

So I keep coming back to one question: Are PIPs really designed to improve performance, or have they become a polite way to manage an exit?

So what should change?

Bring the HR business partner into the conversation when the manager first sees a pattern of underperformance, while the manager is still trying to work out what is happening. HR can ask questions the manager may have missed: What specifically has changed? Has the employee heard clear feedback? Is this a capability problem, an expectations problem or a problem with how the work is being managed?

This one is about PIPs, and the jobs that hang on them. Let's read it slowly.

People were managing poor performance long before the term PIP existed. Managers gave feedback, set expectations and decided whether someone could continue in a role. The formal PIP added written targets, a fixed duration, regular check-ins and documented feedback.

BackgroundWhere the formal PIP came from

There is a reason companies value that documentation. Dismissing an employee can create legal and reputational risk. A written process helps the organisation show what it communicated and what opportunity the employee had to improve. Multinationals brought formal performance policies to India, and Indian companies adopted similar processes.

None of this makes a PIP inherently bad. On paper, the process is transparent. The employee knows what must change and by when. The manager has to provide feedback. HR ensures the rules are followed.

They try a few conversations themselves. Sometimes they avoid giving feedback as directly as they should.

Managing performance is the manager’s responsibility, so it is reasonable for them to handle those early conversations. But why is the HR business partner often absent throughout this period?

Perhaps the manager does not trust the local HR team’s ability to help. Perhaps they think involving HR will make an uncertain problem too formal too soon. Whatever the reason, HR often hears about the case only when the manager says, “I want to put this person on a PIP.”

By then, the manager may have tried everything they are willing to try. They may no longer believe the employee can turn things around. The employee may already sense that confidence has been lost. Most of the bridges are burned before the first PIP meeting takes place.

HR is then brought into the room to explain the policy, record what was said and make sure the organisation follows its process. Those things matter. Termination is an organisational decision, with consequences beyond the manager’s team.

But HR has been brought in to manage the risk of a decision that may already have been made. It is too late for HR to help the manager understand the problem or change their approach. HR becomes part of a tick-box exercise.

Netflix’s keeper test offers an interesting contrast. It asks managers to confront whether they would fight to keep someone on their team. That forces a more direct judgment. But the test does not tell you why someone’s performance fell or whether they can recover. Most companies also cannot resolve every such decision through a quick exit and severance. But hardly any actually spend any amount on say even the training of the individual when they are put on a PIP.

Organisations should also look honestly at their PIP outcomes. How many employees improve and remain in the role? How many resign? How many are terminated? More importantly, when did the manager first notice the problem, when did the employee receive clear feedback, and when did HR become involved?

These numbers should help the organisation examine its process. They should not become targets used to judge individual managers. If managers are penalised for starting PIPs or for unsuccessful outcomes, they will have another reason to hide problems.

I am not saying every PIP is a sham. I am saying that most PIPs I have seen functioned as exit processes. If that is what an organisation’s outcomes show, it should stop congratulating itself on having a well-documented improvement policy.
