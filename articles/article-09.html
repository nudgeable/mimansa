Today's question: if we can't measure training's impact on revenue, what should we measure? Over to the author.

Almost every L&D professional is taught the Kirkpatrick model of measuring training effectiveness.

The model looks simple:

  1. Ask participants whether they liked the training.
  2. Check whether they learned or remembered something.
  3. Measure whether they applied it at work.
  4. Measure the business impact.

The problem begins at Level 4.

· · ·

You cannot say that revenue increased because of one training programme.

People understand this. That is why most organisations quietly stop measuring training impact.

· · ·

So, if you cannot measure the impact of training on revenue, should you continue measuring whether participants liked the content, trainer and facilities?

Absolutely not.

Those scores measure the training experience. They do not measure whether the training changed anything at work.

So if not the feedback form, then what? Here is the question the author would ask instead.

If HR wants to measure training properly, it should work with the participants' managers before the training and ask a much simpler question:

What exact behaviours and actions should these employees start showing after the training?

· · ·

The answer should be specific and observable.

For example:

  • A salesperson visits one more customer every day.
  • A salesperson opens a customer call by asking for the customer's view.
  • A manager holds one coaching conversation every week.
  • A team leader asks for a dissenting opinion before making an important decision.
  • A project manager clarifies roles and responsibilities at the start of every project.
  • A leader gives timely feedback instead of waiting for the annual review.

These are actions that people can take. Managers can observe them. Systems can track them. Participants can confirm whether they completed them.

Where this leaves HR

That approach cannot continue for much longer.

If the measurement approach remains the same, the cycle will simply repeat. But we now have a new challenge from AI. AI will make knowledge even cheaper. CEOs may start diverting traditional training budgets towards AI budgets and ask employees to use AI to learn, practise and solve problems. Hence HR has to change their approach to training.

Hold one question as you read on. Can the person not do it, or will they not?

There is one important distinction that HR must understand. A missing behaviour can come from a skill problem or a will problem.

ToolSkill or Will CheckSort a missing behaviour into skill or will before acting.

Training can help with the skill problem. It can teach someone how to give feedback, conduct a coaching conversation, ask better questions or manage a difficult discussion.

Training cannot create the intent or will to act.

If people understand the behaviour, have received the required training and know what is expected from them, but still do not take action, the problem is no longer with the trainer. It is a management problem.

The manager has to understand why the person is unwilling to act. Perhaps the manager is not role-modelling the behaviour. Perhaps the team does not reward it. Perhaps the person sees no reason to change. Perhaps there are no consequences for continuing with the old behaviour.

HR and the trainer cannot carry responsibility for all of that.

After the training, organisations should use systems and technology to track the specific actions that participants are expected to take. The participant, manager and HR team should be clear about the actions. The assumption is simple: the training should make those actions easier to perform.

Then the organisation should track what happens.

Did the participant accept the action? Did they complete it? Did they repeat it? Did the manager observe any change? Where did the action stop?

This gives HR much better information than a feedback form asking whether the room was comfortable or whether the trainer was engaging.
