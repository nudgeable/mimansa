Now the author goes back 2,000 years, to a man who made a king.

चाणक्यThe parallel · Chanakya and Chandragupta

Indian history already asked the ‘irony in the profession’ question, 2000 years before Ulrich did. Chanakya saw further into human nature, ambition, and loyalty than any king he served. He identified Chandragupta, shaped him, trained him, placed the crown on his head and then stepped back into the shadows of the court, forever the advisor, never the throne. History remembers him as the sharpest strategist of his age. It does not remember him as an emperor. Was that his choice? Or was it simply never his to take?

2000 years later, the CHRO stands in the same place Chanakya was indispensable, unmistakably brilliant at reading power, and somehow still not holding it.

Where the perception came from ?

The stereotype didn't appear from nowhere, and its origin matters, because a perception rooted in real structure is much harder to argue away than one rooted in pure prejudice. When I was about to pick up the Post Graduation course in HR (Personnel Management & Industrial Relations then), one of the Business heads from my erstwhile organisation (Tata Hitachi), advised me against it. The simple reason, “You will never become a CEO”. When I look at what contributed to this in my early years, I realised, HR's own history has conspired to it.

From practiceTata Hitachi, the author's early career

"Personnel departments" emerged to handle payroll, statutory compliance, and the paperwork of hiring and firing and administrative work. Dave Ulrich's 1990s reframing of HR into "business partner" and "change agent" was itself a response to a widely shared perception that HR, as practiced, was mostly filing forms and running the cafeteria of benefits (In the Indian context, Rangolis to be precise). The CHRO title is comparatively young; the wholesale rebranding of "personnel director" into "Chief Human Resources Officer" across companies is largely a 2000s-and-later phenomenon, decades behind the CFO title's arrival in the C-suite.

SourceDave Ulrich's 1990s "business partner" model, as named by the author.

The metrics arrived late. Finance has a shared, audited, universally legible language: revenue, margin, ROI. Every CFO candidate for CEO can point to a P&L they owned and a number they moved. HR, for most of its history, had no equivalent. Engagement scores and attrition rates are real and useful, but neither carries the audited, board-legible weight of an income statement. A CHRO is hardly ever able to say "we reduced regretted attrition by four points, and it will show up in cost-of-hire savings next year.” Even that, is a translation of a people outcome into a financial one, for the room to understand it.

The next part is about who reaches the top, and who does not. Read it slowly.

There's also a harder truth hiding inside what looks like progress: women reach the top of HR in India far more than they reach the top of anything else. An estimated one-third of large-company CHROs in India are women, against roughly 5% of NSE-listed companies with a woman CEO. On one reading, that's real progress. On the harder reading, it's occupational segregation feeding a glass ceiling — women welcomed into the leadership of the function everyone calls indispensable, while the launchpad functions remain far more male- and family-controlled. If the CHRO-to-CEO gap is a business-design problem, it's quietly one of India Inc.'s more significant gender-equity problems too, hiding in a function that looks, on the surface, like a success story.

Why the CHRO can't build their own ladder

If this were purely a competency gap, the fix would be simple; send CHROs to business school, wait a generation. It isn't purely that, and the reasons are structural.

The CHRO role rarely carries revenue or P&L ownership, by design, not by accident. The entire logic of a centralized HR function is to serve every other P&L, not to own one itself. That's a reasonable choice for running HR efficiently and a poor one for producing CEO-ready HR leaders, and very few companies try to solve for both. Board exposure is gated behind the CFO and the CEO, not the CHRO.

I have observed, many a times, that the CHRO typically enters the boardroom for a scheduled compensation-committee item or a succession update, while the CFO is present at every board meeting, every investor call and building the "managing the board" skill, Boards look for in a CEO candidate.

From practiceWho is in the boardroom, and how often

And the sharpest irony of all; the CHRO is very often the executive who designs and administers the company's overall leadership succession framework including, in many companies, the CEO succession plan itself. Yet that same framework, in the overwhelming majority of companies, does not name the CHRO as a plausible candidate. The person who builds the ladder is, structurally, standing outside it. The CHRO spends two decades immersed in what makes other executives "ready," while rarely being formally assessed against that same rubric themselves.

A few did make the climb. What did they do differently?

What the exceptions have in common

The rare CHRO-to-CEO transitions that do exist — Mary Barra, who held HR and manufacturing/engineering roles before running GM; Leena Nair, whose unusually long, board-facing global CHRO tenure at Unilever preceded her move to Chanel, Tanuj Kapilashrami, who became the COO in Standard Chartered from a CHRO role — share one thing, none of them stayed in a pure-HR lane. Each picked up P&L exposure, an operating assignment, or board reps that manufactured the credibility. That is the clearest evidence available that the barrier is addressable, not an immovable truth about what HR people are capable of.
